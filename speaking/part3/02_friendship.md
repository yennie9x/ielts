# Part 3: Friendship

## Discussion Questions & Sample Answers

---

### Q1: What qualities do you think are most important in a friend?

**Sample Answer:**
> In my view, the most important qualities in a friend are honesty, loyalty, and reliability. Honesty is fundamental because a friendship built on pretense or false impressions can't last. You need someone who will tell you the truth, even when it's uncomfortable.
>
> Loyalty is equally important—a true friend stands by you during difficult times, not just when things are going well. And reliability means being someone who keeps their promises and shows up when they say they will.
>
> Beyond these core qualities, I also value having common interests and a good sense of humor. Shared interests give you things to do together, and humor makes the friendship enjoyable. But if I had to prioritize, character traits like honesty and loyalty would come before shared hobbies.

---

### Q2: Do you think it's harder to make friends as an adult?

**Sample Answer:**
> Yes, I definitely think making friends becomes more challenging as we get older, and there are several reasons for this.
>
> First, adults have less time due to work and family responsibilities. When you're a student, you have hours to spend with potential friends, but working adults often struggle to find time for existing friends, let alone new ones.
>
> Second, the natural environments for meeting people decrease. In school, you're surrounded by peers with similar circumstances. As an adult, you have to actively seek out social situations.
>
> Third, adults tend to be more cautious and selective. We've had experiences that make us more guarded about who we let into our lives.
>
> However, the friendships adults do form can be very meaningful precisely because they're chosen more deliberately. I've made good friends through work, and while it took longer than making friends in university, these relationships are based on genuine connection.

---

### Q3: How has social media changed friendships?

**Sample Answer:**
> Social media has transformed friendships in both positive and negative ways.
>
> On the positive side, it allows us to maintain connections with people we might otherwise lose touch with—friends from university, former colleagues, people who've moved abroad. I'm still connected with friends from Chau Doc through Facebook, which wouldn't have been possible before social media.
>
> However, there are downsides. Social media can create a false sense of connection—having hundreds of "friends" online doesn't mean you have deep relationships. People might also compare their lives to curated images others post, leading to feelings of inadequacy.
>
> There's also the concern that online interaction is replacing face-to-face time. Some people spend more energy maintaining their online image than actually spending quality time with friends in person.
>
> Overall, I think social media is a useful tool for friendship when used appropriately, but it shouldn't replace genuine, in-person connection.

---

### Q4: Is it possible to be friends with colleagues at work?

**Sample Answer:**
> Yes, I believe it's possible, and in my experience, some of my good friendships have developed through work. As an IT recruiter, I've met many people professionally who have become friends.
>
> However, there are nuances to consider. Workplace friendships can be complicated by power dynamics—being friends with your boss or subordinate introduces complexities. There's also the risk that professional disagreements could affect the friendship, or vice versa.
>
> I think the key is maintaining appropriate boundaries. You can be friendly and even develop genuine friendships while still being professional at work. It's also wise to have friends outside work so you're not solely dependent on colleagues for social connection.
>
> The benefit of workplace friendships is that you spend significant time together and share common experiences. This can lead to meaningful connections that extend beyond office hours.

---

### Q5: Do you think online friendships are as valuable as in-person ones?

**Sample Answer:**
> This is a nuanced question. I think online friendships can be valuable, but they're different from in-person ones, and each serves different purposes.
>
> Online friendships can offer genuine connection, especially for people who share niche interests that few people around them share. Through gaming, for example, I've connected with people I'd never meet otherwise. These relationships can provide support, shared experiences, and meaningful conversation.
>
> However, in-person friendships offer things that online ones can't fully replicate—physical presence, shared experiences in the real world, non-verbal communication, and activities together. There's something irreplaceable about sitting across from a friend over a meal.
>
> I wouldn't say one is universally better than the other. For some people, especially those who are geographically isolated, online friendships might be lifelines. But ideally, most people benefit from having at least some close in-person friendships alongside any online ones.
