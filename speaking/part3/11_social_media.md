# Part 3: Social Media & Internet

## Discussion Questions & Sample Answers

---

### Q1: What are the advantages and disadvantages of social media?

**Sample Answer:**
> Social media offers significant advantages. It enables connection—I stay in touch with family in Chau Doc and friends from university largely through social platforms. It allows people with niche interests to find communities. It's become essential for businesses to reach customers and for professionals to network. And it provides platforms for social movements and information sharing.
>
> However, the disadvantages are equally significant. Social comparison leads to decreased well-being as people compare their reality to others' curated highlights. Echo chambers form when algorithms show us content matching our existing views. Misinformation spreads easily. Privacy is compromised as platforms collect extensive personal data. And many people develop unhealthy relationships with these platforms—checking compulsively and struggling to disconnect.
>
> I think the net effect depends on how individuals use social media. Intentional, limited use that focuses on genuine connection can be positive. Passive scrolling, comparison, and excessive time spent tend to be negative. The responsibility lies with both users and platforms—users to manage their usage, platforms to design for well-being rather than just engagement.

---

### Q2: How has social media affected how people communicate?

**Sample Answer:**
> Social media has fundamentally changed communication in several ways.
>
> First, it's made communication more frequent but often shallower. We might interact with more people, but many interactions are brief likes or comments rather than meaningful conversation.
>
> Second, it's become more public. Conversations that once were private now often happen where others can see. This changes what people are willing to say and creates performative elements to communication.
>
> Third, written and visual communication have increased relative to voice and face-to-face. This can lead to misunderstandings since text lacks tone and non-verbal cues.
>
> Fourth, communication has become more asynchronous. We can respond when convenient rather than in real-time. This creates flexibility but sometimes delays or avoids direct conversation.
>
> Fifth, it's expanded who we communicate with beyond immediate physical circles, connecting people across geography and culture.
>
> I think there's been both enrichment and loss. We're more connected globally but sometimes less connected locally. We communicate more but perhaps with less depth. The challenge is maintaining meaningful communication amid all the noise.

### Q3: Should social media companies be responsible for the content on their platforms?

**Sample Answer:**
> This is a complex question at the intersection of technology, free speech, and responsibility.
>
> On one hand, platforms host content—they don't create it. Holding them responsible for everything users post would be practically impossible and might lead to excessive censorship or even make platforms unviable.
>
> On the other hand, these companies profit from engagement, including engagement with harmful content. Their algorithms can amplify dangerous misinformation, hate speech, or harmful material. When their design choices contribute to harm, some responsibility seems appropriate.
>
> I think a middle ground makes sense. Platforms should be responsible for: removing clearly illegal content, preventing amplification of demonstrated misinformation, enforcing their own stated policies consistently, and being transparent about their moderation decisions.
>
> They shouldn't necessarily be liable for every user post, but their active choices—what to recommend, what to amplify, what to allow—should carry some accountability.
>
> The challenge is implementation. Who decides what's harmful? How do we balance free expression with preventing damage? These require ongoing societal negotiation and clear legal frameworks that many countries are still developing.

---

### Q4: Do you think children should use social media?

**Sample Answer:**
> I believe children should have limited and supervised access to social media, with age-appropriate restrictions.
>
> Young children probably shouldn't use social media at all. They lack the cognitive development to understand what they're seeing, navigate social dynamics online, or protect themselves from predators and harmful content.
>
> Teenagers are more complicated. Social media is deeply integrated into peer relationships now, and completely restricting it may socially isolate teens. However, adolescent brains are particularly vulnerable to social comparison, addiction patterns, and cyberbullying.
>
> I think reasonable approaches include: delaying access until a certain age (many platforms require 13+, though enforcement is weak), supervising usage especially initially, having ongoing conversations about online safety and healthy usage, modeling good digital habits as adults, and limiting screen time.
>
> Platform design also matters. Features that encourage comparison or enable anonymous harassment are particularly harmful to young users. Platforms could do more to design for young people's well-being.
>
> The goal shouldn't be complete protection from the digital world—that's impossible and counterproductive—but gradual, guided introduction that builds digital literacy and healthy habits.

---

### Q5: How might social media evolve in the future?

**Sample Answer:**
> Social media continues to evolve rapidly, and several trends might shape its future.
>
> Platform diversity may increase. TikTok's rise shows that established players aren't invincible. We might see more specialized platforms for different communities and uses rather than one-size-fits-all giants.
>
> Content formats will likely keep shifting. Video, especially short-form video, dominates now. Virtual reality and augmented reality could create more immersive social experiences.
>
> Privacy concerns might lead to changes. Regulations are increasing, and users are becoming more aware. We might see platforms that offer better privacy protections, or decentralized social networks that don't rely on centralized data collection.
>
> AI integration will deepen. Content creation, moderation, and recommendation will increasingly involve AI, raising questions about authenticity and manipulation.
>
> There may also be a counter-movement. Some people are reducing social media use or seeking alternatives that prioritize well-being over engagement. Platforms might eventually compete on healthy design features.
>
> I hope future social media better serves genuine connection rather than just engagement metrics, but that will require both user demand and potentially regulatory pressure.
